Running an AI-first workshop

Ten principles for teaching people to build with AI — what we learned shipping a workshop that takes a seller from nothing to a published agent, and how to rescue a room when it goes sideways.

The old workshop contract was: follow these steps exactly, and if you fall behind, you are lost. The AI-first contract is different — the participant can be wrong and still arrive, because the artifacts steer them back. That single shift is what these principles protect.

The ten principles

01The person steers, the AI executes

The participant's job is to say what outcome they want and judge what comes back. Every command, file edit, and deployment step belongs to their AI. If your agenda has a slide where forty people type the same thing, you have built a typing class, not a workshop.

Why Typing is where rooms diverge. One typo in row three and that person spends the rest of the session behind, quietly, and doesn't tell you.

02Count the commands, and keep the count honest

Count the commands a participant must run, then publish the number. Ours is two: the install one-liner, and a one-time pac auth create sign-in the first time they ever authenticate. The one-liner has a path that avoids it entirely. Everything else is conversation or a button — and if the count creeps to three, something in the design is wrong, not something in the room.

Why Every additional command multiplies the ways a room can fragment — wrong shell, wrong directory, wrong OS, a copied line that lost its newline.

03Make the artifact its own on-ramp

The file you hand out should contain the instructions for its own setup, written for an AI to read. Ours opens with a block addressed to whatever assistant receives it: check whether the server is running, install it if not, put me in this folder, confirm I loaded, then tell the person what to say next.

A participant with nothing installed can drag the file into GitHub Copilot Chat, say “set this up for me,” and arrive at the same place as everyone else.
Why It collapses the entire setup phase into an action people already know how to do — drag a file into a chat.

04Auto-steer, don't restart

People arrive half-configured and mid-stream. The artifact should diagnose the actual state and fix it from there — server missing, server down, agent not loaded, loaded but the person is lost, or a step that failed halfway. Each state gets its own next action.

Why “Start over” is where people quit. Diagnosis costs the AI a few seconds and saves the participant their dignity.

05Meet them on their machine

Detect the platform and show one line — the Windows user should never scroll past a macOS command wondering if it matters. Same for anything else you can infer.

Why Every irrelevant instruction on screen is a decision you handed someone who came to learn, not to choose.

06Never show an empty room

A participant who scrolls before reading should see a working example, clearly labeled as one, not blank panels. Ours loads a default agent and says “Example shown — pick yours above.”

Why Blank space reads as broken. People assume they missed something and stop, instead of scrolling on.

07Verification with computed answers

Every library stack ships test questions whose expected answers are computed from its synthetic data — a specific name, a specific ranking, a specific refusal. Not “check that it responds sensibly.” First-party agents ship none, by design: Microsoft documents their validation, so the workshop links that instead of inventing its own.

A wrong answer becomes visible, not arguable. That is the difference between a demo and a liability.
Why Rooms cannot judge quality by feel, and neither can the person demoing this to a customer next week.

08State the boundary instead of faking the capability

When the marketing promised something the build cannot honestly do — autonomous action, real-time telemetry that does not exist — write the boundary into the agent's instructions rather than simulating it, and say so out loud. A declined capability with a stated reason survives a customer conversation; a simulated one does not.

Why The faked capability always surfaces in front of the customer, and it takes the credible parts down with it.

09Carry the friction, don't hide it

Our deployment playbooks contain the actual failures we hit and the actual fixes: the security-role error, the CLI defect that crashes after a successful publish, the sync order that silently erases your work. The AI reads them and applies the fix instead of guessing.

Why The failure a room hits is almost never novel. Writing it down once turns a twenty-minute derailment into a sentence.

10Honest reporting, always

Instruct the AI to report what actually happened, including failures, and never to present an unverified step as complete. Then hold yourself to it in the room: if a check fails, say so and fix it in front of people.

Why A participant who catches you glossing over a failure stops believing the parts that worked.

What we would tell another team

Building this surfaced three lessons that generalize past our subject matter.

Test the artifact, not the demo. We ran the whole path end to end repeatedly and found bugs no demo would ever surface — an agent name that routed to the wrong stack, a share link the page could not read back, a button that told people to pick something they had just picked. Every one of those would have hit a room instead.

Let an independent reviewer attack it. We ran repeated adversarial passes against the live site, and several of the worst problems in the later rounds were regressions introduced by the fixes in the earlier ones. You do not catch that by re-reading your own work — and the fix is a regression test per defect, not a more careful re-read.

Write the doctrine down where the AI can read it. The difference between an AI that helps and an AI that improvises is whether you gave it your actual procedure — including the parts where things break. That document is the real deliverable; the slides are a summary of it.
